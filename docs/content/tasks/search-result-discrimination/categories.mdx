# The three categories

A category says where the results came from and who decided what was relevant.
It is a reported dimension rather than a detail, because a model can read web
snippets well and multi-paragraph Wikipedia badly, and one averaged number
would hide it.

## The grade scale

Every candidate carries a grade on TREC's four-point scale:

| Grade | Meaning |
| :---: | --- |
| 3 | perfect — answers the question |
| 2 | highly relevant |
| 1 | **related but does NOT answer it** |
| 0 | not relevant |

Grade 1 is the interesting one, and it is NIST's own wording:

> Passages were judged on a four-point scale of Not Relevant (0), Related (1),
> Highly Relevant (2), and Perfect (3), where 'Related' is actually NOT
> Relevant — it means that the passage was on the same general topic, but did
> not answer the question.

That sentence is why this benchmark uses TREC at all. The distinction between
*on topic* and *answers it* is the whole task, and a person already drew it.

A candidate counts as supporting evidence at grade 2 or above. Grade 1 never
does.

## `web` — 171 items

**Source:** TREC Deep Learning track 2019 and 2020, passage judgements over the
MS MARCO collection. 57 questions, each built at all three noise tiers.

The non-relevant candidates here were *retrieved for this query* by the systems
that competed in the track, then read and rejected by an assessor. Nobody had
to invent distractors: they are what a real retrieval system actually returned.

Example, `srd-web-dl19-19335-easy` — question: **"anthropological definition of
environment"**. Five candidates: three graded relevant, two graded 0, one of
which is about zeolite in aquariums and another about environmental *science*
as a discipline. The second is the hard one.

:::note[The passages are not in this repository]

MS MARCO's terms grant non-commercial research use and explicitly extend no
licence, so this repository does not redistribute its text. The `web` and
`no_answer` items are built on your machine from your own download — about
twenty seconds after `make data` — and a committed manifest of ids and
checksums proves your build matches the published one. See
[building](./building#why-part-of-the-set-is-built-on-your-machine).

:::

There is no gold answer for this category. TREC judged relevance, not answers,
so answer correctness is not scored here; relevance is.

## `wikipedia` — 120 items

**Source:** HotpotQA's distractor split. Ten paragraphs per question, two
carrying the supporting sentences and eight retrieved as near neighbours of the
question.

These questions need **two** candidates joined together, which is a different
skill from picking one good result:

> **What team had Tony Roberts replaced by an American sportscaster from
> Buffalo, New York when he retired?**

One paragraph identifies Tony Roberts. Another identifies the man from Buffalo
who replaced him. Neither alone answers it.

110 of the 120 are HotpotQA's `bridge` type — follow a link from one fact to
the next — and 10 are `comparison`.

The grades here are **derived**, not judged: HotpotQA records which paragraphs
its supporting sentences came from, so those become grade 3 and the rest 0.
There is no middle. Every item records `label_source: derived` for exactly this
reason, and a derived label is weaker evidence than a human relevance
judgement. The two are never averaged into one number.

This category does have gold answers, so it is where answer correctness and
groundedness get measured.

## `no_answer` — 40 items

**Source:** TREC again — 40 queries held back from the `web` category, using
only their grade-0 passages.

Nothing is fabricated and nothing is deleted to make these. Every candidate in
a `no_answer` item was read by an assessor against this exact question and
graded **0**. Grade 1 passages are excluded too, since "related but does not
answer" is the grey area this category is not about.

The right response is that the results do not answer the question. A model that
answers anyway is either hallucinating or answering from memory, and both are
failures of the thing being measured.

A question never appears in both `web` and `no_answer`. A model that had
already seen the answerable version would recognise the question instead of
reading the candidates, which is the exact behaviour this category is trying to
catch.

## What the shipped file looks like

```json
{
  "id": "srd-wiki-5ab6e6c75542",
  "category": "wikipedia",
  "question": "What team had Tony Roberts replaced by an American sportscaster from Buffalo, New York when he retired?",
  "candidates": [
    {"id": "1", "text": "Eli Gold (born December 15, 1953) is an American sportscaster…", "title": "Eli Gold", "source_id": ""},
    {"id": "3", "text": "Tony Roberts (born 1928) is an American retired sportscaster…", "title": "Tony Roberts (sportscaster)", "source_id": ""}
  ],
  "relevance": {"1": 0, "2": 0, "3": 3, "4": 3, "5": 0},
  "supporting_ids": ["3", "4"],
  "gold_answer": ["Notre Dame Fighting Irish football"],
  "noise_tier": "easy",
  "label_source": "derived",
  "source": "hotpotqa",
  "source_id": "5ab6e6c75542992ae4cf7f5f",
  "subcategory": "bridge-hard",
  "rationale": "HotpotQA marks which paragraphs its supporting sentences came from…"
}
```

The model is shown `question` and `candidates` only. `relevance`,
`supporting_ids` and `gold_answer` are what it is scored against.

Candidate order is a seeded shuffle. In every source the relevant passages
arrive first, and a model that learned "the answer is near the top" would score
well without reading anything.
